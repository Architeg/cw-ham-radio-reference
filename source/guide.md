# How to use this guide {#using}

A first Morse contact can be quite small: two call signs, a signal report, perhaps a name, and a friendly goodbye. Once you've done that, the same few building blocks will carry you through a surprising amount of radio.

This guide gives you both a way in and something to keep beside the key. Read the operating chapters when you're learning. Open the tables when you need a word, a signal, or a reminder. Experienced operators can go straight to the abbreviations, Q signals, phrase bank, and final desk card.

## Choose your route

| What you want to do | Start here |
|---|---|
| Make a first contact | [Quick start](#quick), then [Your first contact](#first) |
| Learn by ear and by hand | [Rhythm](#rhythm), then [Keys and a simple station](#keys) |
| Look up something you heard | [Abbreviations](#abbreviations), [Q signals](#qsignals), or [Procedure signals](#procedure) |
| Practice without transmitting | [Practice](#practice) and [Using the web](#web) |
| Keep a reminder within reach | [The desk card](#deskcard) |

## Reading the examples

`YOURCALL` means your authorized call sign. `THEIRCALL` means the other station's call sign. Replace the whole word; neither is sent literally. Example names, locations, reports, power levels, and frequencies are just that: examples.

`[AR]`, `[BT]`, and `[SK]` are joined procedure signals, often called **prosigns**. The brackets tell you to send one continuous character. You don't send the brackets. Ordinary groups such as CQ, DE, and TNX retain their normal letter spacing.

Dots and hyphens show the sound pattern. A space separates letters in written code. A slash between words in an exercise can stand for a word gap; a real slash inside a call sign is a character of its own, `-..-.`.

Examples use international Morse and plain English. You can complete a routine contact with a small vocabulary. You can also send full words whenever they make you easier to understand.

## A worldwide starting point

National rules decide where you may transmit, how you identify, and what your operating privileges allow. Band plans and event rules add their own practical details. Check the rules that apply where the transmitter is located. Listening, practicing with an oscillator, and learning on the web give you useful things to do before you have permission to transmit.

The operating advice here is aimed at ordinary amateur contacts. Formal message handling, emergency services, and specialized clubs may use additional procedures. When you join an organized activity, listen to its exchange and read its rules.


# 1. Quick start: one contact at a time {#quick}

## Get ready

Have your call sign, a clock, and somewhere to write. Paper is fine. Set a sending speed at which you could understand an answer. Before transmitting, check the band, operating mode, antenna connection, and permitted power.

Find someone calling CQ at a comfortable speed. Listen long enough to get their complete call sign and learn what kind of contact they want. A relaxed CQ is a good first choice. A fast contest exchange is a different conversation.

## Answer a call

When the caller finishes and listens, send:

> `THEIRCALL DE YOURCALL YOURCALL K`

Now listen. If the answer contains your call sign, you're in. If it names someone else, give that station room to finish.

## Start your own call

Listen on a suitable clear frequency. Ask:

> `QRL?`

Wait for an answer. Ask once more if you're unsure. If someone says the frequency is in use, move. If you've heard no activity or reply, try a short call:

> `CQ CQ DE YOURCALL YOURCALL K`

Leave a real listening gap. Another operator needs time to tune, recognize the call, and answer.

## Give the essentials

> `THEIRCALL DE YOURCALL GE [BT] UR RST 559 559 [BT] NAME ALEX ALEX [BT] QTH RIVERDALE RIVERDALE [BT] HW? K`

That says: good evening; your report is 559; my name is Alex; my location is Riverdale; how do you copy? Substitute your own name and location, and the report you actually mean to give.

## Four requests worth learning early

| Send | You mean |
|---|---|
| `UR CALL AGN PSE` | Please repeat your call sign |
| `NAME AGN PSE` | Please repeat your name |
| `MY RST AGN PSE` | Please repeat the report you gave me |
| `PSE QRS` | Please send more slowly |

## Finish and write it down

> `TNX FER QSO 73 THEIRCALL DE YOURCALL [SK]`

Record the other call sign, UTC date and time, band or frequency, CW, and both reports. Name and location are useful extras. A missing weather report isn't a problem; an uncertain call sign is worth asking about while the other station is still there.


# 2. Hear the rhythm {#rhythm}

Morse is a set of sound patterns with carefully placed silences. Those silences tell you where a character ends and a new word begins. Even a slow contact becomes easier when the spacing is steady.

## The timing underneath it

| Element | Standard length |
|---|---|
| Dot, or dit | 1 unit |
| Dash, or dah | 3 units |
| Gap inside a character | 1 unit |
| Gap between characters | 3 units |
| Gap between words | 7 units |

A dash is one longer sound. Three separate dots are a different character. The same distinction applies to silence: a letter gap isn't just an optional hesitation.

Use the printed patterns to check a character, then spend time hearing it. If you're counting every dot and dash, reduce the amount of text or give yourself longer gaps. Try recognizing the whole shape of a letter instead.

## Character speed and text speed

Words per minute, or WPM, is commonly based on the reference word PARIS. One standard word occupies 50 timing units, including the final word gap. At standard spacing, a dot lasts `1200 / WPM` milliseconds. At 20 WPM, that's 60 milliseconds.

With **Farnsworth spacing**, the characters keep a brisk, recognizable shape while the gaps between characters and words grow longer. A trainer set to 20 WPM characters and 10 WPM effective speed gives your ear a clear sound and your brain more thinking time. Trainers label these settings differently, so check which value changes the characters and which changes the gaps.

**Koch practice** starts with a small character set and adds characters as your recognition improves. Koch and Farnsworth describe different choices; you can use them together.

## Keep moving when you miss a character

Leave a blank and catch the next one. It feels untidy at first, but chasing one lost letter often costs you the next five. During a contact, a short request for the missing field is more useful than trying to reconstruct a whole paragraph.

As familiar groups become recognizable, let them stay together: CQ, DE, NAME, TNX. You may eventually hear a word without consciously spelling it. There's no deadline for that. Writing things down remains a useful operating skill.

## Listen to your own sending

A recording is a patient practice partner. Send a few short words, stop, and play them back. Check that the letters have room to breathe. On a hand key, listen for dashes that shrink as your hand tires, or spaces that disappear near the end of a word.

Raise the speed when the present speed feels settled. A clean exchange at a modest pace is a contact you can enjoy.


# 3. Keys, sound, and a simple station {#keys}

You can learn a great deal with a key and a practice oscillator. The oscillator turns a contact closure into a local tone; no radio signal needs to leave the room. A transceiver adds the receiver, transmitter, and antenna needed for an on-air contact.

## What each type of key does

| Tool | What your hand controls |
|---|---|
| Straight key | The duration of each dot, dash, and space |
| Sideswiper, or cootie | Manual characters made with sideways movements |
| Mechanical bug | Automatic mechanical dots; manually timed dashes |
| Paddle with an electronic keyer | A keyer makes the dot or dash timing when you operate the paddle |
| Keyboard or memory sender | Stored or typed text becomes keyed characters |

A paddle and a keyer are different things, even when the keyer is built into the radio. A dual-lever paddle also doesn't tell you which keying mode is selected. Iambic modes A and B differ in what happens when a squeeze is released; try them on a local tone and use the documented setting that feels comfortable.

For a straight key, adjust the contact gap and return force so that you can send without gripping or straining. Rest your arm comfortably and take breaks. With a bug, make sure the mechanical dots are stable before adding hand-made dashes. Choose manageable practice text rather than forcing speed with tension.

## Connect by function, not by plug size

A KEY socket, a microphone socket, and an audio output can all use similar-looking plugs. Their wiring and electrical limits may be completely different. Check the manuals for both devices before connecting them. An older transmitter may place voltages on the keying circuit that a modern electronic keyer cannot handle directly.

Test the local tone with transmitting disabled first. Check straight-key versus paddle mode, contact polarity where relevant, and the intended tip/ring connections. A stuck carrier is often a wiring or mode problem, not a reason to keep experimenting on a busy frequency.

## Set up the receiver

Start with a comfortable audio pitch and a filter broad enough to find the signal. Once tuned, narrow the filter if it helps separate nearby stations. A very narrow filter can also hide an off-frequency reply or cut off a drifting signal.

The tone you hear is an audio difference produced by the receiver. A 600 Hz listening tone isn't a 600 Hz radio frequency. Use the radio's documented CW tuning indication or receive offset so that your transmitted signal lands where the other operator expects it.

A sidetone lets you hear your own sending. Set it to a comfortable level. It should help your timing without making you miss the return to receive.

## On-air CW and practice tones

In ordinary CW radio operation, the transmitter keys its carrier on and off. Feeding an audio tone into an FM voice channel is another way of carrying Morse information, but the receiving mode and occupied signal are different. A practice oscillator, an internet Morse room, and a CW radio contact can all teach timing; keep track of which kind of connection you're making.

## A small offline kit

A hand key, oscillator, pencil, clock, and a page of exercises make a useful starting kit. Add a receiver when you want real signals. Add software when it solves a particular problem for you. None of the exercises in this guide depends on a particular brand of radio or decoder.

Punctuation is sparse in many radio contacts. Short blocks, sensible pauses, and [BT] often do most of the work. The question mark is especially useful, but a named request such as NAME AGN PSE is clearer than a question mark by itself.

Some programs support extra symbols or national characters. Confirm the alphabet at both ends before relying on them. International call signs should be entered into a log with their proper Latin letters and digits, including any relevant slash suffix.

## Cut numbers

In a numeric exchange, you may hear N for 9 and T for 0. A sometimes stands for 1. Thus **5NN** is a shortened 599. These are conventions used in context, not replacements for the normal alphabet.

Send full digits while you're learning. Don't substitute cut numbers into a call sign. If you're unsure whether you heard a letter or a shortened number, ask for the field again.

## Useful listening contrasts

Practice A/N, U/D, V/B, K/R, and F/L in mixed groups. For E/I/S/H/5, pay attention to the length and rhythm of the whole character. For T/M/O/0, do the same with dashes. Mix the groups often enough that you must listen rather than remember their order.


# 5. Procedure signals: who sends next {#procedure}

Procedure signals make the conversation easy to follow. Learn a few well and use the rest as you encounter them.


|Signal | Pattern | Meaning|
|---|---|---|
|K | `-.-` | Your turn; an invitation to transmit|
|[KN] | `-.--.` | The named station is invited to reply|
|[AR] | `.-.-.` | End of a message|
|[SK] | `...-.-` | End of the contact|
|[BT] | `-...-` | A separator between thoughts|
|[AS] | `.-...` | Wait|
|[VE] | `...-.` | Understood, in procedural use|
|[HH] | `........` | An error: eight dots|
|[SOS] | `...---...` | Distress signal; not an on-air practice call|

## K, [AR], and [SK] do different jobs

Finish a general call with K so that someone knows you're listening. During a contact, K hands the conversation back. [KN] makes the intended recipient explicit.

[AR] marks the end of a message. [SK] marks the end of the contact. You don't need to string every signal together at the end of every transmission. For a first contact, a clear call sign exchange, K, and a clean closing will take you a long way.

You will see joined characters printed with a bar above them, in angle brackets, or with no special formatting. This guide uses square brackets consistently. KN is also heard as separate letters. The joined [KN] pattern is identical to an opening parenthesis; its position in the exchange tells you which meaning is intended.

## BK and CL

BK normally has a letter gap: `-... -.-`. It can mark a short handover once both operators know who's talking. Some operators join it, so expect variation when listening.

CL is sent as ordinary letters and means the station is closing. A station that signs off with CL may not be listening for another call. Give them the chance to leave.

## Fix a sending error

Stop, give [HH] if useful, and repeat the word or group from the beginning. For a small obvious slip, a pause and a clean repeat may be enough. Repeat a mistaken call sign in full rather than sending one unexplained letter.

The familiar two E characters sometimes exchanged after goodbye are an informal farewell. They aren't required to complete a contact. In a biography or club notice, SK may instead mean Silent Key, a radio amateur who has died; that is a different use of the letters.

## Keep ordinary letters separate

CQ, DE, TNX, TU, and 73 are regular groups. Their familiarity doesn't remove the spaces between their characters. Joined prosigns are the exceptions you deliberately learn.


# 6. Your first contact, with a worked example {#first}

## Pick an easy conversation

Choose a signal you can hear steadily and a speed you can follow. Listen through a complete exchange if possible. You'll learn the caller's rhythm and find out whether they're chatting, working an event, or handling a queue.

Write down their full call sign before you call. If you use a decoder, compare its text with what you hear. A plausible-looking call sign on a screen can still be wrong.

## The exchange

In this example, **A** is THEIRCALL, a station calling CQ. **B** is you, YOURCALL. Sam and Alex are example names. The reports differ on purpose.

### 1. A calls

> `CQ CQ DE THEIRCALL THEIRCALL K`

A has asked for a contact and switched to receive.

### 2. B answers

> `THEIRCALL DE YOURCALL YOURCALL K`

This makes the addressee clear and gives A a second chance to copy your call.

### 3. A gives the first information

> `YOURCALL DE THEIRCALL GE [BT] UR RST 579 579 [BT] NAME SAM SAM [BT] QTH HILLTOWN HILLTOWN [BT] HW? [KN]`

A has given **you 579**, introduced Sam, and sent a location. Write down the parts you need. If you missed the name, ask for it; there's no need to abandon the contact.

### 4. B replies

> `THEIRCALL DE YOURCALL R TNX SAM [BT] UR RST 559 559 [BT] NAME ALEX ALEX [BT] QTH RIVERDALE RIVERDALE [BT] HW? [KN]`

You give **A 559**, then your own name and location. R says that the preceding information was received. If only some of it was received, say what you copied and ask about the missing part instead of confirming everything.

### 5. A continues

> `R ALEX [BT] HR PWR 10 W ANT DIPOLE [BT] WX CLOUDY [BT] [KN]`

This is an invitation to a little conversation. You can describe your station, ask a question, or politely finish. A weather report is optional.

### 6. B closes

> `R SAM [BT] TNX FER QSO ES UR PATIENCE [BT] 73 THEIRCALL DE YOURCALL [SK]`

You're thanking Sam and ending the contact.

### 7. A says goodbye

> `TU ALEX 73 YOURCALL DE THEIRCALL [SK]`

That's the exchange. There's no need to add another long transmission.

## The log entry

| Field | In this example |
|---|---|
| Other station | THEIRCALL, replaced with the actual call sign |
| Report sent | 559: your assessment of A's signal |
| Report received | 579: A's assessment of your signal |
| Name and location | Sam, Hilltown |
| Date, time, frequency | The actual UTC values and operating frequency |
| Mode | CW |

The names and towns here are invented training data. Don't copy them into a real log. You can shorten the conversation considerably once you understand what each block does.


# 7. Calling, answering, and having a conversation

## A short CQ leaves space for an answer

A useful call gives people enough time to notice you without making them wait through a long announcement. CQ two or three times, your call sign a couple of times, then K is a reasonable place to start. Conditions may justify a little more repetition.

After the call, listen for a reply that may be slightly off your receive frequency. An extremely narrow filter can make an answer easy to miss. Don't keep restarting CQ so quickly that a slower operator never gets in.

## When you answer

For a relaxed contact, THEIRCALL DE YOURCALL K identifies both stations clearly. In a busy DX queue or contest, the normal answer may be just your complete call sign once. Match the situation you've been listening to.

Listen for the answer addressed to you. If it names another station, stay quiet. If the operator is trying to complete a partial call that clearly isn't yours, help by leaving them alone.

## Choosing a topic

Once you've exchanged names and reports, pick one thing to talk about. An antenna someone built, a portable outing, a new key, or a recent repair can become a good conversation.

> `I BUILT A DIPOLE THIS WEEK [BT] WHAT ANT ARE U USING? [KN]`

Keep the first question short. Give the other person room to respond. You can always follow up once you know how well they are copying.

A long first transmission containing every detail of your station can be hard work for a newcomer. One or two thoughts at a time usually makes for a more enjoyable exchange.

## A quick handover

After the contact is established, short BK exchanges can feel quite natural:

A: `BK HR PWR 5 W BK`  
B: `BK FB HR 10 W BK`

Continue to identify as required by your local rules. Return to full call signs if there is confusion or another operator joins.

## Calling after another contact

Wait until both stations have finished. Then call the station you want. A clear closing doesn't always mean the operator wants another contact; CL is a particularly good hint that they're leaving.

To join a conversation already in progress, wait for a suitable gap and give your call briefly. Then wait for an invitation. In a roundtable, make the next recipient clear so that several people don't all start together.

## Directed calls

A directed CQ asks for a particular region, group, or type of contact. Respect it. CQ DX generally seeks distant stations, but what counts as DX depends on the band, location, and caller's intention. It isn't a universal distance or a simple border crossing.


# 8. Repeats, slow sending, and difficult signals {#repeats}

A request for a repeat is part of a normal contact. The most useful request tells the other operator exactly what you need.


|What happened | A useful response|
|---|---|
|You missed the call sign | `UR CALL AGN PSE`|
|You copied the report but lost the name | `RST CPY [BT] NAME AGN PSE`|
|The sending is too fast | `PSE QRS`|
|You need a slower, stated speed | `PSE QRS 10 WPM`|
|Letters are clear but words run together | `PSE MORE SPACE BETWEEN WORDS`|
|You missed the report they gave you | `MY RST AGN PSE`|
|A word disappeared in fading | `LAST WORD AGN PSE`|
|You need a short pause | `[AS]`|
|They copied your call incorrectly | Repeat your complete call sign clearly|
|The contact is no longer readable | `SRI NIL CPY TNX 73 [SK]`, if you can send|

## Acknowledge only what you have

R ALL OK is a strong statement. If you have the report but not the name, say so. That lets the other operator repeat one field instead of guessing where you became lost.

If someone asks you for NAME AGN, send the name clearly, perhaps twice. Increasing the speed or repeating the entire preceding transmission makes their job harder.

## When the receiver is struggling

Check the tuning before changing everything else. Try a filter that reduces nearby signals without cutting off the one you want. Set comfortable gain and volume. Very loud audio may make listening tiring and can overload a decoder input.

Fading, interference, uneven hand sending, and two stations on top of each other can all confuse decoding software. Treat the decoded line as a clue. Ask for an uncertain call sign or report again.

## When nobody answers

Check the transmit frequency, antenna connection, mode, and power setting. Check whether the station is operating split. Then try another suitable caller or another time. Hearing someone well doesn't guarantee they can hear you equally well.

A spot on a website is useful for finding a station, but it doesn't prove that the station is still there or listening where you expect. The current on-air instructions matter most.

## When a station disappears

Listen for a while, then try a brief addressed call if appropriate. If there's no return, end the attempt. Mark uncertain or incomplete data in your notes. An earlier conversation, a callbook entry, or a cluster listing can't supply words you didn't receive.


# 9. Good manners on a shared band {#manners}

The best courtesy is often simply leaving space: space between words, space for a reply, and enough room for someone else's contact.

## Before occupying a frequency

Listen, then ask QRL?. You may hear only one side of an existing contact. A quiet moment doesn't necessarily mean the frequency is free. If someone replies that it is occupied, find another spot without starting a debate.

Avoid tuning up over a conversation. Use a suitable dummy load for tests that don't need an antenna, or follow your radio's documented short tuning procedure on a clear, permitted frequency at an appropriate power.

## Make the other operator's job easier

Send a complete call sign clearly. Leave useful pauses. Slow down when asked. Keep your blocks short if the other person is working hard to copy. A little patience is more memorable than a fast exchange full of uncertainty.

If you need to stop, say so and close. If the other operator has a queue or limited battery time, let a short contact stay short. Neither of you owes the other a long conversation.

## Treat mistakes as mistakes first

Someone may have the wrong VFO selected, miss a directed call, or misunderstand the turn-taking. A brief courteous correction can help. Adding an angry carrier, repeated dots, or insults only makes another signal for everyone to fight through.

Before asking someone to move, check your own receiver. If a nearby signal makes the contact difficult and there is room elsewhere, moving may be the easiest answer.

## In a pileup

Send your call once and listen. Let the called station finish with the person they selected. Use your whole call, rather than a suffix or a power category. Follow requests for a particular area or partial call. The operator handling the queue needs a chance to hear an answer.

## A scheduled contact is still on a shared frequency

An arrangement to meet, or sked, is a plan rather than a reservation. If the frequency is busy, use an agreed alternative or find a clear one. A website spot also doesn't confer ownership.

## If you hear a newcomer

Try a shorter sentence, a little extra word spacing, or a slower reply. Repeat the important field without fuss. You can help someone have a successful contact without turning it into an examination.

## Real distress

Distress signals deserve attention and should never be used as casual on-air exercises. If you hear an apparent immediate threat to life, avoid causing interference, copy the location and message, note the time, and follow the emergency procedures available in that situation. Where an official emergency service can be reached directly, use it. Amateur radio is one possible communication path, not a guarantee of a response.


# 10. Reports, numbers, frequencies, and time {#reports}

A signal report describes the signal you are receiving. It isn't a measurement of the other transmitter's advertised power, and it doesn't have to match the report you receive.

## RST at a glance

| Part | Range | What you're judging |
|---|---|---|
| R: readability | 1-5 | How well you can make out the message |
| S: strength | 1-9 | The received signal's strength |
| T: tone | 1-9 | The quality of the keyed tone |

## Readability


|R | Practical meaning|
|---|---|
|1 | Not readable|
|2 | Only occasional characters or words get through|
|3 | Readable with substantial effort|
|4 | Readable with little difficulty|
|5 | Fully readable|

## Strength


|S | Practical meaning|
|---|---|
|1 | Barely perceptible|
|2 | Very weak|
|3 | Weak|
|4 | Moderate|
|5 | Fairly good|
|6 | Good|
|7 | Fairly strong|
|8 | Strong|
|9 | Very strong|

## Tone

The historical T scale describes roughness and ripple in the tone. It runs from a very rough, broad note at T1 to a clean tone at T9. T2-T4 describe increasingly less rough notes; T5-T6 still have obvious ripple; T7-T8 approach a clean note with progressively less residual modulation.

A weak signal can still have a clean tone. Many sound modern transmitters merit T9, even when their signals are faint. If the issue is a chirp or key clicks, say what you hear rather than inventing an unfamiliar number.


|Report | How to read it|
|---|---|
|599 | Fully readable, very strong, clean tone|
|579 | Fully readable, fairly strong, clean tone|
|559 | Fully readable, strength 5, clean tone|
|339 | Readable with effort, weak, clean tone|
|5NN | A shortened 599 in context|

An S-meter can help, but readings depend on calibration and receiver settings. Use a report that communicates something useful. In many contests, 599 functions as a conventional part of the exchange; in a relaxed contact, an honest report is more informative.

## Other scales are different

QSA uses a formal strength scale of 1-5. QRI has a formal tone scale of 1-3: good, variable, bad. Neither should be silently substituted for S or T in an RST report. For an ordinary CW contact, RST is usually the simpler choice.

## Keep units clear

A frequency written as 14060 normally means 14060 kHz, or 14.060 MHz, in the relevant HF context. Include KHZ or MHZ when it could be misunderstood. An audio pitch such as 600 Hz is a separate receiver setting.

PWR 5 W reports five watts. ABT 5 W says approximately five watts. Describe the power you know or have selected; don't turn a product label into a claimed measurement.

Use UTC for scheduled contacts and logs unless you explicitly agree otherwise. A clock showing UTC avoids a lot of arithmetic. When converting from local time, check the date too: near midnight, the UTC date can differ from the local date.

Temperatures need a unit when it matters. TEMP PLUS 12 C and TEMP 54 F are clear. Distances and antenna heights also benefit from KM, M, or another explicitly stated unit.


# 11. Abbreviations A-F {#abbreviations}

Look up the group you heard, then read the example in context. These are shared habits, not a requirement to abbreviate every word. Full words are welcome when they are clearer.

## The context does some of the work

RPT can ask for a repeat or refer to a report. FM may mean from in a sentence and frequency modulation in a technical discussion. R acknowledges reception; C or YES is a clearer affirmative answer to a question. If the intended meaning is uncertain, ask or use a full word.

# 16. DX, queues, and split operation {#split}

DX operating can be a brief exchange with a distant station or a relaxed conversation across a long path. When many people call one station at once, the queue is called a pileup.

## Listen for the pattern

Before joining, copy the station's call sign, its exchange, and its instructions. Does it send on one frequency and listen on another? Is it asking for a region or a particular partial call? Is it taking short reports or having longer contacts?

Send your complete call once, then listen. If you don't get a reply, wait for the next invitation. Repeatedly calling over the person who was selected slows the whole queue down.

## A split example

Imagine a station transmitting on 14.025 MHz and sending UP 1. You listen on 14.025 and call on 14.026 MHz, provided the frequency is permitted and otherwise usable.

| Your setting | Example |
|---|---|
| Receive | 14.025 MHz: hear the station |
| Transmit | 14.026 MHz: call where it listens |
| Operating mode | CW |
| Before keying | Check both frequencies and the split setting |

These are illustrative frequencies, not a recommendation to occupy them. UP 1-3 announces a listening range one to three kHz higher. UP by itself doesn't state an exact offset; listen for clarification and for where the successful callers are being answered.

RIT usually changes only reception. XIT changes the transmit offset. SPLIT commonly assigns different tuning channels to receive and transmit. Confirm how your particular radio implements them before calling. If you can't set the required transmit frequency, wait for a suitable opportunity rather than calling on top of the station's own signal.

## A brief exchange

A: `YOURCALL 599`  
B: `R UR 579 TU`  
A: `TU THEIRCALL UP`

That's enough when the operator is working a queue and has asked for that exchange. If your call was wrong, correct it before the contact ends. Save the station description for a more relaxed conversation.

## Timing beats persistence

A readable signal in a listening gap has a better chance than a long call nobody can separate from the noise. Keep track of the operator's rhythm. Respect a reply to someone else. A short pause is useful operating time.


# 17. Clubs, portable activities, and contests {#activities}

Organized activities give you a reason to get on the air and people to look for. They also bring their own exchanges and equipment rules. Read those before treating a familiar-looking contact as an entry for an award.

## Portable contacts

An operator on a hill, in a park, or at a temporary station may have a queue, a small battery, and changing weather. A short report and a friendly goodbye can be exactly right. If they invite a conversation, you're free to stay a little longer.

Some programs use a location reference. Copy it completely when you need it, including the prefix and punctuation used by the program. Ask REF AGN PSE if it disappeared in fading. A nearby landmark on a map isn't enough to invent the reference.

Different programs handle logs differently. Some rely on the activator's log, some allow separate participant submissions, and award rules vary. Your own accurate station log remains useful in every case.

## Key-based clubs

A club may encourage straight keys, bugs, or sideswipers and set limits on electronically generated sending for its awards. The physical appearance of a paddle doesn't tell you whether the transmitted timing is manual. Check the actual key policy, including any accessibility provisions.

You can enjoy a conversation with a member without claiming to meet a club's award conditions. If asked for a membership number you don't have, say I AM NOT A MEMBER. Don't make one up.

## Contests

A contest exchange might include a serial number, zone, section, age, region, or another field. There is no universal number that works for all events.

Here is an example only for an event that requires RST and a serial number:

A: `CQ TEST THEIRCALL`  
B: `YOURCALL`  
A: `YOURCALL 5NN 023`  
B: `5NN 007`  
A: `TU THEIRCALL TEST`

023 is A's number. 007 is yours. You don't echo their serial number back as your own. Ask NR AGN PSE if you missed it.

Before entering, check the operating period, allowed bands, category, exchange, repeat-contact rules, and report format. A logging program helps once it is set up for the right event. A paper log is still a useful fallback.

## Keep it friendly

An event is a shared activity. Let the active operator set the pace. Ask briefly when you need a repeat, and give the requested information. If you want a slower practice contact, a club practice session or an arranged meeting may be a better fit than the busiest part of a contest.


# 18. Logs and confirmations, on paper or online {#logs}

A log is the record of what happened. A confirmation is a separate acknowledgment of that contact. Keeping those two ideas apart makes both simpler.

## What to write during the contact


|Field | Why it matters|
|---|---|
|UTC date and time | Identifies when the contact happened, including the date at midnight|
|Other call sign | The actual station, including relevant suffixes|
|Frequency or band | Where you worked|
|Mode | CW for these examples|
|Report sent | Your report of the other signal|
|Report received | The report they gave you|
|Name and location | Useful conversation details|
|Event exchange or reference | Fields required by the activity, when applicable|

A pencil and ruled notebook work well. Leave separate boxes for sent and received reports. If you're busy, write the essential fields first and tidy the notes later.

## Keep the direction straight

If you give 559 and receive 579, record **sent 559 / received 579**. The words sent and received are less ambiguous than two unlabeled numbers. They are also clearer than a lone S, which might mean either sent or signal strength.

## Afterward

Check uncertain details while they are still fresh. A callbook or online lookup can help you spot a likely typo, but it can't establish a call sign you never copied. Keep a note of the uncertainty rather than silently filling the gap.

If you use logging software, export a backup in a common interchange format such as ADIF. Check a sample of the export for call signs, UTC dates, modes, frequencies, and reports. An export file isn't a confirmation service; it is a way to move your records.

## Ways to confirm

A paper QSL card, an electronic matching service, or an event's log system may provide the acknowledgment you want. Choose what suits the activity. A confirmation accepted by one award program may not be accepted by another.

A service may compare records submitted independently by both stations. Your own upload can therefore remain unconfirmed until the other station contributes a matching record. Uploading the same entry everywhere doesn't guarantee every program will recognize it.

QSL during the contact often just means received. It isn't a promise that an online record has already matched. Ask about confirmation arrangements politely and follow the recipient's published instructions.

## Shared and remote stations

Use the call sign and station identity authorized for the operation. A club station's records belong in the club's agreed logging process. If a receiver or transmitter was remote, record the relevant station location and check the activity's rules about remote assistance and receiving sites.

A remote receiver can be excellent for learning, but reception at that site should not be described as reception at your home antenna.


# 19. Practice that fits your life {#practice}

## A fifteen-minute session

| Time | Exercise |
|---|---|
| 3 minutes | Familiar letters and digits, mixed |
| 4 minutes | Short words or call signs |
| 4 minutes | One small contact scenario |
| 2 minutes | Send a short passage and listen back |
| 2 minutes | Note the two things to revisit next time |

That's a suggested routine, not a deadline. Regular sessions are easier to sustain when they end before you're exhausted. Keep an ordinary record of what improved and what still needs work.

## An entirely offline route

Use an oscillator and a key. Ask a partner to send a few words from this book without telling you which ones. Record a short session if you can, so that both of you can listen again. Swap roles and change the names, reports, and numbers each time.

A receiver adds the challenge of real timing, noise, and unfamiliar operators. Try copying one call sign and a report before attempting a whole conversation. You can learn from a contact without being able to copy every word.

## If you use a trainer

Look for adjustable character and effective speeds, random groups, a way to repeat a passage, and a clear record of errors. Browser, desktop, phone, and dedicated hardware trainers can all provide useful practice. Choose a tool you can use regularly rather than collecting several you rarely open.

If a program accepts text, start with a few phrases from the phrase bank. Listen with the text hidden, write what you heard, and compare afterward. For a joined prosign, use the program's documented prosign syntax. Typing separate A and R won't necessarily produce [AR].

## If you use a decoder

Try listening first and checking the screen second. A decoder can expose a missed word or help you explore faster material. It can also teach you to trust an incorrect display if you never check it.

Adjust audio level and tuning before blaming your ears. Several signals at once, uneven spacing, and fading can all degrade the result. On air, ask for a repeat rather than treating an uncertain decoded field as confirmed.

## Move from rehearsal to conversation

Arrange a short contact with an operator who knows your level. Agree on UTC, a starting frequency, and an alternative if it is occupied. Say what you can comfortably receive. Asking for a practice contact is more useful than promising a speed you can't yet follow.

You are ready for that arranged contact when you can send your call, copy basic numbers, ask for a repeat, request QRS, and recognize the closing. You don't need the entire dictionary memorized first.


# 20. Use the web when it helps {#web}

The web can supply practice audio, a receiver in another location, a clue about who is active, or a place to keep a log. Those are different jobs. Decide which one you need before choosing a site.

## A receiver in your browser

A web-accessible receiver takes its signal from an antenna at the receiver's own location. You select a supported frequency and mode, start the audio, and listen. Many also show a waterfall, which is a useful visual map of signals over time.

You don't need to attach your own radio or antenna to listen this way. You do need to remember where that receiving antenna is. A station heard there may be inaudible at home, and the reverse can also be true.

Use CW mode when available, or a suitable SSB setting according to that receiver's instructions. Begin with a usable filter width, find the tone, and refine the tuning. Some browsers require a click before audio will play.

## A spotting network

A spot says that a person or an automatic receiver detected a station at a particular time and frequency. It is a lead to follow. It isn't the received audio, a guarantee of current activity, or proof of a two-way contact.

A practical sequence is: find a likely station, tune a receiver, listen to the instructions, and decide whether you can make the contact. The spot helps you find the conversation; your receiver tells you what is happening now.

## Online practice and keyed chat

An online trainer can send text as audio. A networked Morse room may let participants exchange keyed timing. A key often needs an interface supported by that particular system; a plug that fits doesn't establish compatibility.

Internet practice can be social and useful, especially when you have no local partner. Label it honestly in your notes. A network exchange alone isn't an amateur RF contact or an automatic entry for a radio award.

## Monitoring your own signal

With permission to transmit, you may be able to hear your signal through a remote receiver. Allow for network delay and differences in propagation. Delayed audio isn't a good replacement for a local sidetone when you're trying to time your hand movements.

An automatic spot can also provide feedback that a receiving node detected your call. No spot doesn't prove that the transmitter was silent: reception, coverage, recognition, and network operation all play a part.

## Optional examples

The reference section lists a few training, receiver, spotting, and logging resources. They are starting points, not required accounts or endorsements. A notebook, a receiver, and a practice partner remain a complete way to work through this guide.

# 22. Small exercises, with answers {#exercises}

## Pick your response

1. Someone asks QRL? while you are already using the frequency.
2. You received the report but not the name.
3. Your correspondent is sending faster than you can copy.
4. They call another station whose suffix resembles yours.
5. They copied your call with the wrong final letter.
6. The station says UP 1 and your transmit and receive frequencies are identical.
7. You gave 559 and received 579. Which number goes in each log field?
8. A decoder suggests a call sign, but you're unsure about the digit.
9. The agreed frequency for your sked is busy.
10. A station asks for a club number you don't have.

## Answers

1. Briefly indicate that the frequency is occupied: QRL or C is sufficient.
2. RST CPY, then NAME AGN PSE.
3. PSE QRS; add a comfortable speed if helpful.
4. Keep listening until the other station has finished.
5. Repeat your whole call sign clearly.
6. Set and check the appropriate permitted transmit frequency before calling.
7. Sent 559; received 579.
8. Ask for the call again.
9. Use a clear alternative; the arrangement wasn't a reservation.
10. Say I AM NOT A MEMBER.

## Read this block

> `R TNX FER RPT [BT] HR PWR 10 W ANT DIPOLE [BT] WX RAIN TEMP PLUS 12 C [BT] PSE QRS [KN]`

The sender received your report, uses ten watts and a dipole, has rain and a temperature of twelve Celsius, and would like you to slow down. The reply is directed to the named station. The block doesn't give a new report of your signal or a location. Don't add either to the log by inference.

## Repair this call

> `CQ QRZ QRL DE YOURCALL 599 SK`

These groups do different jobs. Listen and ask QRL? separately. When the frequency is clear, call CQ CQ DE YOURCALL YOURCALL K. QRZ? asks who called you; a report follows contact with an identified station; [SK] closes a contact.

## Make the rehearsal unpredictable

Take a short exchange and change the name, town, report, and power each time. Ask a partner to alter one field without warning. You'll find out whether you are receiving the information or replaying a memorized script.

# 24. The desk card {#deskcard}

## Before you key

Listen. Copy the complete call. Check the mode and transmit frequency. Send at a speed you can receive.

## Find room or answer

`QRL?` — Is this in use? Listen before calling.  
`CQ CQ DE YOURCALL YOURCALL K` — A short CQ.  
`THEIRCALL DE YOURCALL K` — A clear answer.

## Give a first report

> `THEIRCALL DE YOURCALL GE [BT] UR RST 559 [BT] NAME ALEX [BT] QTH RIVERDALE [BT] HW? K`

Replace the report, name, town, and call signs with the real information.

## Get back on track

`UR CALL AGN PSE` — Repeat your call.  
`NAME AGN PSE` — Repeat your name.  
`MY RST AGN PSE` — Repeat the report you gave me.  
`PSE QRS` — Send more slowly.  
`[AS]` — Wait.

## Close and log

> `TNX QSO 73 THEIRCALL DE YOURCALL [SK]`

UTC date · UTC time · call sign · band or frequency · CW · report sent · report received.

**Leave space for the reply. Ask for what you missed. Enjoy the contact.**
